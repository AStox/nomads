   - **Count.** Every basin lies in an ice-scoured valley; the erosion landscape makes 3 to 7 closed hollows on land and at most one is deep enough for a lake. The basement carries 57, 65 and 63 valley segments (a segment is one 2 to 5 km draw of the scour loop). 70% of them hold a basin; a half (either side of the sill) that holds a basin holds 2.3 of them on average (149 basins in 64 halves on seed 1), so a valley does not make just two, it makes one wherever its constant-depth floor, which follows the ground, dips below the walls its two ends leave and below the ground a crossing surface valley lowers. On the 24 seeds the lakes of 5 ha or more are 28 on 257 km2 (0.109 a km2) against Bornholm's 18 assessed lakes on 588 km2 (0.031 a km2), 3.6 times; lake area is 3.7 times. The two ratios are the same, so the lakes' size is not what is wrong.
   - **Size.** The mean lake is 2.8 to 3.0 ha and the mean of those of 5 ha or more is 7.3 to 7.7 ha; the 18 lakes Bornholm's municipality assesses hold 93.03 ha, 5.2 ha each (R1353). The lakes are the right size and there are too many.
   - **Depth.** The basins are deep (median 11 to 16 m below the spill) because the floor is cut up to 50 m with an abrupt wall at each end, but one cell wide, a deeper cut lengthens the lake along the valley and does not widen it, so depth acts on area only through length.
   - **The water balance does not limit anything.** The kernel fills 97 to 100% of the cells of every basin, including the 3 to 6 m ones (0.89 to 1.00 by depth class). The rain fix did not move this: a basin holds water whenever its catchment brings more than the open-water evaporation of one cell or two. The balance is a possible limit that is never reached.

2. **Why the valleys close, and what Bornholm's do.** The code cut each valley to a constant depth below the local ground, which follows the ground, then stopped it at an end cell at full depth, so a valley across a slope ends against a wall as high as its depth less the slope's fall. The sill in the middle (cut falling to nothing, as R528 describes) leaves each half with a closed end at both of its own ends. Trap (R1350, the Landscape section on streams, lakes and mires and the one on the joint valleys) says the opposite of closed: the basement's streams "follow the fracture valleys' course" for kilometres (Kampeløkke å for 4 km, Møllebæk through Skrubbedal, Spælinge å in Døndal, Kelseå in Kløvedal), the island's north-east streams run almost at right angles to the coast because the valleys do, and "long joint valleys that cross the whole granite terrain, like Ekkodalen-Kløvedal, have a sill in the middle that is the watershed, so the valley system holds two streams with different flow directions". A valley half drains out of its end. The island's lakes are "narrow, elongated, often in line with one another" (Nydam and Gamledam; the kilometre-long Dammemose), small, under 1% of the land, and "joint-valley lakes arisen where the clearing of the material has been so large that the floor lies below the groundwater table"; in Paradisbakkerne's crossing valleys and the western Almindingen they stand thick. So a joint-valley lake is a local overdeepening in a valley that otherwise drains, not every dead end.

3. **What can overdeepen a floor, and by how much (R1351).** Alley et al. (2003) take the long profiles of the beds of highly erosive glaciers and find them tending to steady-state angles opposed to, and "slightly more than 50 per cent steeper than", the ice surface slope, so that "additional subglacial deepening must be enabled by non-glacial processes". A floor the ice cleared can therefore rise toward its outlet no steeper than 1.5 times the ice sheet's surface slope. The surface slope at Bornholm is Nye's plastic profile's (R1352): dE/ds = tau / (rho g H) with H^2 = 2 tau (distance from the margin) / (rho g), which gives sqrt(tau / (2 rho g x)) at x from the margin.

4. **The fix.** Each valley is cleared as before (the same draws, depth and sill), then its floor is drained out of both ends: it comes out at the ground at each end, and anywhere along it it lies no lower than the floor beyond it toward an end less the adverse slope times the distance to it. The cut is only ever made shallower. What the ice and its meltwater can overdeepen is what is left, and the basins that remain are where that bound lets a floor dip below its spill by 3 m or more.

## The law

For the cells k = 0 to n-1 of one valley, in order, with z_k the ground (the erosion landscape), d_k the distance in m from cell k-1 to k (d_0 = 0), t_k the place along it from 0 to 1, deep the draw and sill the cell nearest t = 0.5:

- the ice's floor f_k = z_k - deep min(1, |t_k - 0.5| / 0.15) on the basement and f_k = z_k elsewhere. [R531 for the 50 m draw's bound, R528 for the sill; the draw, the 15% and the 1.5 km spacing are [design], unchanged]
- the drained floor g is the lowest floor not under f that satisfies g_0 >= z_0, g_(n-1) >= z_(n-1), g_k >= g_(k-1) - s d_k for k up to the sill, g_k >= g_(k+1) - s d_(k+1) from the end down to the sill, and at the sill the higher of its two halves; g is two running maxima, one from each end. [R528, R1350 for the drainage, R1351 for the bound; the floor coming out at the ground at each end is [design]]
- the scour is z_k - g_k where positive. [as before]
- the bound s = 1.5 sqrt(tau / (2 rho g x)) with tau = 90 kPa, rho = 917 kg/m3, g = 9.81 m/s2 and x = 300 km: 1.5 times 0.41%, 0.61%. [R1351 for the 1.5, R1352 for the form and tau; rho, x [UNVERIFIED], below]
- the basin rule and the 3 m water table are unchanged (floor more than 3 m below the spill, R522, R530, R528).

The valley's own axis stands for the ice's and the meltwater's flow along it, and the same bound is applied toward either end, though the ice came mostly from the north-east (the ice-worn knolls face NE, Trap, as the earlier rock note read it; the MIS 2 readvance overrode the horst, R532): [design]. A valley the ice crossed at an angle meets the bound along its own axis only in part.

## What is closed, partly closed, open

- **Closed:** the named gap, lake area (1.68% to 0.45%, Bornholm's 0.45%) and lakes of 5 ha or more (3.6 times to 1.4 times Bornholm's 18 per km2). The basins are now 3 to 9 m below their spill (median 4 to 5), where they were 11 to 16 m; no depth for Bornholm's lakes was found [gap]. Every basin is still an ice-scoured valley's, as in R528.
- **Made worse by closing it:** the water table. Within 150 m of a valley cell the median table was 3.8 m (seeds 1 to 4, before) and is 2.6 m; 150 to 375 m 1.75 m before and 0.88 m after; 375 to 750 m 0.62 m and 0.34 m; beyond 1.5 km from any valley both are 2.1 m. The old valleys, cut 23 m on average, and the 1.7% of lake (a fixed head) were the aquifer's deep drains; the table elsewhere has always been near the ground [INFERENCE: the bands are measured, that the drains are why is read from them]. With the valleys shallower and the lakes fewer the wet band the water-table note put to the rock's transmissivity and the recharge cap (its gap 2) is wider. So the median depth falls 1.72 to 1.02 m, the table at the ground 7.3 to 11.0% of land, marsh 13.2 to 17.6%, peat 8.9 to 12.5%, trees 65.6 to 62.1%, and the carbon off peat, which follows the off-peat set, 67 to 84 t/ha. These are the aquifer's gaps, not touched here; the lakes' fix removed a mask on them.
- **Streams, which this did not aim at, fall from 0.76 to 0.45 km/km2** (Bornholm 0.71, R534; seeds 1 to 3: 0.76, 0.67, 0.71 to 0.45, 0.38, 0.48). - **Open:**
  - The inputs of the bound (the shear stress, the margin's distance, the ice's density) and the 1.5 are the one place the number of lakes follows; see the sweep. A better reading of the ice's thickness over Bornholm (Lambeck et al. 2010) is the next thing to find; a low basal shear stress such as an ice stream's (20 kPa, [INFERENCE]) gives a bound of 0.29% and lakes under 0.1%.
  - Overdeepening that is not the ice's (meltwater under pressure, tunnel valleys, R1351) is not modelled, and neither are the flat ice-scoured basins in weak granite (Bastemose's kind, R1350) or lakes in the Almindingen plateau's crossing valleys; the lakes here stand wherever the basement's valley floors dip. Their sizes cannot be set by Herman et al.'s law (R1356: the exponent is 0.65 to 2.6 and the sliding speed unknown [gap]), so the depth draw stays [design].
  - The valleys are 2 to 5 km, the longest real one 12 km (R1354), and each ends at the ground; the valley network's real lengths and its crossings (a valley ending in a crossing valley, not in the ground) would keep more of the drain.
  - Bornholm's 0.45% is all water; the 18 assessed lakes are 0.16%, eight named as mires and six gravel pits. At most three to five are natural open lakes, so the share that is the ice's may be under the model's.
  - The lake balance fills every basin (94 to 100%); it has not been tested with a basin that should stay empty.
