## Laws and constants

### Equilibrium line from temperature and precipitation (R1462)

The equilibrium line is where the annual surface balance is zero. Ohmura and Boettcher show the P/T curve of Ahlmann is the energy balance written in water equivalent: ablation at the equilibrium line equals the year's accumulation, and ablation is set by the June to August mean air temperature T (December to February in the south), which loses its near-surface gradient at the surface and so is taken from the free atmosphere. Fits to 104 glaciers [cited]:
- P = 5.87 T^2 + 230 T + 966 (standard error 648 mm); P = 264.1 T + 957 (650 mm); with the summer global radiation S (W/m^2) P = (2.2 S - 130) T + 3.12 S + 122 (510 mm). P is the year's precipitation at the equilibrium line in mm, T in C.
- 9% of the year's total precipitation at the ELA falls as liquid on average; the free-air lapse rate used to move a station to the ELA is 0.65 K/100 m; the liquid share is 8% per K; 75% of the melt energy is longwave plus sensible heat (the longwave emissivity of the sky 0.84; sensible flux gradient 7.9 W/m^2/K); the melt season at the ELA runs only 10 C between -2 and +8 C [cited].
- Used as a mass balance [derived]: the balance at height z is b(z) = 0.91 P_solid(z) - A(T_JJA(z)) where A(T) = 5.87 T^2 + 230 T + 966 mm/yr is the ablation the quadratic implies, and T_JJA(z) = T_JJA,sl - Gamma z. A is 966 mm at 0 C, 1,700 at 3 C, 2,900 at 7 C and falls to zero near -4.8 C [derived]; one degree of summer warming moves the ELA up by 1/Gamma = 154 m (at 6.5 K/km); a 10% change of precipitation of 1,700 mm moves it by about 100 m [derived].
- ELA against latitude, from the table of R1462 (92 of its glaciers parsed here): median 2,838 m at 45 to 55 degrees (n 27, 1,828 to 3,330), 1,435 m at 55 to 65 (n 29, 790 to 2,025), 973 m at 65 to 90 (n 21, 335 to 1,489), 3,725 m at 35 to 45 (n 6), 5,737 m at 29 N (Himalaya) and 5,441 m at 16 S (the Andes), and 150 to 200 m for the Antarctic (63 to 66 S); at 50 N the coastal Garibaldi glaciers (Sentinel 49.9 N 1,828 m, Helm 2,082 m, Place 2,257 m, summer T 7.5 to 5.2 C) lie 600 to 1,000 m below the interior Rockies (Ram River 51.9 N 2,838 m, T 2.95 C) [cited data, grouped here].
- Gravity and the lapse rate: Gamma = g/c_p for dry air; at g = 3.1 m/s^2 every ELA would be 3 times higher in metres (atmosphere file); keeping g = 9.81 keeps these numbers.

### Ice flux, shallow-ice approximation (R1463)

Mass conservation dh/dt = m - div(h U), h the ice thickness and m the surface mass balance; the depth-averaged velocity from Glen's law is U = -(2/(n+2)) A (rho_i g)^n h^(n+1) |grad s|^(n-1) grad s, s the surface height, n = 3, A = 1e-16 Pa^-3 yr^-1 (3.2e-24 per second, about the value at 0 C [UNVERIFIED: Cuffey and Paterson's 2.4e-24 is remembered]), rho_i 910 kg/m^3, g 9.81 [cited; the paper prints the factor as 2/(n+1) in one place and 2/(n+2) in its radial form; the latter is the standard one]. In years and metres: flux q = Gamma h^5 |grad s|^2 grad s with Gamma = 2 A (rho_i g)^3 / 5 = 2.85e-5 m^-3 yr^-1 [derived]; 200 m of ice on a slope of 0.1 moves at 46 m/yr. Ice of 373 m on a slope of 0.03 has a basal shear stress of 100 kPa, the usual ceiling of valley glaciers [derived; the 100 kPa is remembered, UNVERIFIED]. The scheme is diffusive with D = Gamma h^5 |grad s|^2: 6.9e5 m^2/yr at 300 m and slope 0.1, so the explicit step limit dx^2/(4 D) is 52 years at 12 km and 0.1 year for a 2 km ice sheet on a slope of 0.02 [derived]: stiff; see the kernel section. Cold ice is stiffer than temperate by a factor of 10 to 100 [INFERENCE: Arrhenius dependence not read]; ice sheets of 2 to 3 km thick are cold-based over most of their area, and erosion needs sliding, so only temperate, wet-based ice cuts the bed.

### Glacial erosion (R1460, R1461)

e = K_g |u_s|^l, e the erosion rate of the bed, u_s the sliding speed, K_g a constant, l the exponent. Both papers' fits [cited]:
- R1460 (Franz Josef Glacier, New Zealand, one glacier's surface velocities by stereo imagery up to 3 m/day, and suspended sediment of about 10 mm/yr): l = 2.02, K_g = 2.7e-7 (units m^(1-l) yr^(1-l)), a good fit; l = 1, K_g = 1e-4 fits the integrated erosion as well; a spatially integrated erosion and velocity cannot separate K_g from l (they trade off). The nonlinear law agrees with the abrasion theory (the drag force on the debris times the rate it is dragged). Earlier field estimates, in Alaska, the Alps and Patagonia, gave l = 1 and K_g near 1e-4 and most landscape models use them.
- R1461 (38 glaciers): erosion varies by five orders of magnitude, and by up to a factor of 100 at a given sliding speed; the regression of erosion on sliding gives l = 0.65 (R^2 0.54, p < 0.01; 0.69 with the Meserve outlier) and K_G = 1e-4 (erosion in m/yr). Latitude explains nothing (R^2 -0.11); precipitation does (R^2 0.42, p < 0.01), temperature little; erosion above 10 mm/yr needs a mean annual air temperature above about -2 C and precipitation above about 2,500 mm, nearly all Alaskan; the exponent is under one, consistent with larger cavities beneath faster ice and with hard-bed sliding theory.
- Compared: for sliding speeds of 50 and 500 m/yr the rate ratio is 10^2.02 = 105 with l = 2.02 and 10^0.65 = 4.5 with l = 0.65; at 365 m/yr (1 m/day) l = 2.02 gives 40 mm/yr, l = 1 gives 36.5 mm/yr and l = 0.65 gives 4.6 mm/yr [derived]. The default for the planet [design]: Cook's global l = 0.65 and K_G = 1e-4, with Herman's l = 2.02 as the sensitivity; a failing check that depends on the choice is a gap, not a reason to choose.
- Sliding speed taken from the modelled ice [design]: the shallow-ice model gives deformation only; add Weertman-type sliding u_s = K_s tau_b^m with tau_b = rho_i g h |grad s| (m about 3) and K_s a free coefficient, or take u_s from the flux budget as R1460 does (the ice flux of the balance less the deformation flux, divided by thickness) [gap: K_s and m are not read]. Sliding only where the bed is at the melting point: mean annual temperature above -2 C as the active-erosion flag [derived from R1461's pattern].
- Sediment: the erosion is carried to the sea; it feeds Unit 3's shelf (sediment density 2,500; R1411).

### Fjords below the lowstand (R1465, R1411 and the Wikipedia fjord article read for the examples)

- Rivers cannot erode bedrock much below sea level; kilometre-deep fjords, tens of km long, are made by ice (R1465, abstract). In Kessler et al.'s model, topographic steering of ice towards passes and erosion proportional to ice discharge are enough: kilometre-deep fjords through a coastal range within a million years by a positive feedback (more ice, faster, more erosion); the deepest fjords are through the highest topography and drain most of the interior ice [cited]. The pattern of the Wikipedia article: fjords form best in ranges against the prevailing westerlies where orographic snow feeds the glaciers (west Norway, Puget Sound to Alaska, southwest New Zealand, Chile) [UNVERIFIED, secondary]; Sognefjord reaches 1,300 m below sea level and has a sill 100 to 200 m deep at the mouth [UNVERIFIED, secondary].
- Flotation limit [derived]: grounded ice cuts only while rho_i h > rho_w d, so the cut depth d <= (rho_i / rho_w) h = 0.89 h (910 over 1,028 with sea water); 1.5 km of ice can cut a fjord 1.3 km deep, which matches Sognefjord's depth [INFERENCE]. The sill follows from the thinning of ice as it spreads at the mouth (lower erosion rate), which the sliding law gives unaided.
- Sea level in the ice age: lowstand at the last glacial maximum 134 m below today (R1464); a fjord is cut when the coast is inside the ice, which stood on the shelf. Isostatic loading: Airy gives ice of thickness h depressing the crust by h rho_i/rho_m = 0.28 h (1 km of ice, 280 m of depression), relaxing as exp(-t / tau_r), tau_r = 4 pi mu / (rho g lambda) (R1411 eq. 6.105): 4,400 yr for lambda 3,000 km, and 12,000 yr for lambda 1,000 km (the planet's ice sheets are smaller, so slower rebound) [derived from the cited law; mu 1e21 Pa s].
- Resolution: a fjord is 2 to 10 km wide, which tier 0 (12 km) cannot hold; carving needs a tier of 1.5 km or finer (three or four quadtree levels) in glaciated coastal cells, and the 75 m window is the final scale [INFERENCE].

### Sea level and ice volume (R1464, R1411)

- ice-volume-equivalent sea level: delta_zeta = -V_ice rho_i / (rho_w A_ocean) [cited form, derived]: Lambeck's 52e6 km^3 of additional grounded ice at the LGM over 3.62e14 m^2 of ocean (R1411 App. B) gives 132 m, against the paper's -134 m [derived check]. Lambeck: a rapid fall of about 40 m in under 2,000 yr at 30 ka; a slow fall to -134 m from 29 to 21 ka; deglaciation 16.5 to 8.2 ka at 12 m/ka on average (about 120 m, 45e6 km^3), with a peak of at least 40 mm/yr at 14.5 to 14.0 ka (meltwater pulse 1A); a rise of about 4 m in the last 6.7 ka; nothing above 15 to 20 cm in any 200 yr over the last 6,000 [cited]. The last interglacial 4 to 8 m above today [cited]. R1411 sec. 4.24: if all polar ice melted the sea would rise about 80 m; on 10^7 to 10^8 yr the ridge volume gives hundreds of metres.
- The planet [derived]: ocean area 0.71 x 5.03e13 = 3.6e13 m^2, so 130 m of ice-equivalent needs 5.1e6 km^3 of ice, 10 times less than the Earth's 52e6 (ocean areas scale with R^2); on the planet's 1.46e13 m^2 of land that is an average ice cover of 350 m, or 1.5 km thick over 23% of the land, as the Earth's LGM (about 27%).
- Hydro-isostasy: water of depth delta loads the sea floor and presses it down by delta rho_w/rho_m = 0.30 delta, so a relative sea level change at a coast is smaller than the ice-volume-equivalent (Airy) by about 30% over the deep sea and by less on shelves [derived; the full gravitational fingerprint is [gap], R1464 computes it with a viscoelastic Earth].

### Orbital forcing (R1438, R1466)

Periods: eccentricity about 100 and 400 kyr (405 kyr main, +-0.012 [UNVERIFIED]); precession 19 and 23 kyr (about 21); obliquity 41 kyr between 22.1 and 24.5 degrees [cited R1438 for the periods, the rest UNVERIFIED]. Amplitude of the signal: the 65 N summer insolation at 115 kyr was 35 to 40 W/m^2 below today's [cited, R1438 Fig. 3]. Early Pleistocene ice volume followed 41 kyr, the last million years 100 kyr, which orbital forcing alone does not explain (R1438 cites the mid-Pleistocene transition as unresolved). For the planet the orbit and tilt are inputs [design]: obliquity oscillation eps(t) = eps_0 + 1.2 degrees x sin(2 pi t / 41 kyr + phase) and a precession-eccentricity term e sin(varpi) with e cycling 0.0019 to 0.06 and varpi at 21 kyr, with the phases drawn from the seed [analogue: Earth's values]. A slow cooling of the baseline (the Cenozoic trend of Earth's climate) is [gap]: which of the 41 and 100 kyr regimes the planet shows depends on thresholds in the ice model, not on a law read here.

## What a kernel needs (Unit 6)

State per cell: bed elevation (from the crust thickness of Unit 2 less the ice load, i32 m Q8), ice thickness (i32 m Q8), surface s = bed + h, temperate flag, sliding speed (Q16 m/yr), erosion rate (Q16 mm/yr) with the bed lowered in the crust-thickness state (a loss of rock d lowers the crust by d: tectonics file), sea level (a scalar, Q16 m), the ELA field (m) from the atmosphere, mass balance from section 1 (Q16 mm w.e./yr). Inputs per month from the atmosphere: T_JJA at sea level and its lapse, P and its solid share at each height. Order: orbital forcing (scalar) to the atmosphere's seasonal insolation, then ELA and mass balance, then the shallow-ice step, then sliding and erosion, then sea level from the ice volume (sum of V_i) and the isostatic response. Steps: the explicit shallow-ice limit is dx^2/(4D), 52 years at 300 m and 0.1 year for thick ice; with 300 kyr to run (three cycles) this is too many dispatches under the 5 minute rule, so use (a) a semi-implicit step (Picard or ADI) with 5 to 20 yr, (b) a cap on flux, or (c) a lumped ice sheet model on the coarse tier and valley glaciers only at fine tiers [design, gap]. The outer loop of orbital steps is 1 to 5 kyr; the erosion and isostasy at 50 to 500 yr. Counter-based randomness: only the phases of the orbit.
