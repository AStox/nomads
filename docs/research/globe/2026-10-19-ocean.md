## Laws and constants

### Wind-driven gyres (R1451)

- Sverdrup balance: where friction and the curl of the flow's own relative vorticity are small (the interior, away from boundaries), beta V = curl_z(tau / rho_0): the meridional transport is M_y = curl_z(tau) / (rho_0 beta); the volume transport of a basin is N = (1/(rho_0 beta)) integral over x of curl_z tau dx [cited, R1451]. beta = 2 Omega cos(phi) / a, 1.98e-11 per m per s at 30 N on Earth (cited as beta_0).
- Observed numbers [cited, R1451]: annual-mean wind-stress curl in the North Atlantic subtropics -0.5 to -1.0e-7 N/m^3 (stronger toward the west), gyre interior transport -2.5 to -5.1 m^2/s, basin transport -28 Sv observed and -30 Sv from the long-term stress curl; 1 Sv = 1e6 m^3/s; largest mean stress about 0.25 Pa in the Southern Ocean west of Australia.
- Closure: the net transport through a zonal section vanishes in steady state, so the interior Sverdrup flow is returned by a western boundary current of width of the order of the baroclinic deformation radius (O(100 km) in the subtropics, R_d about 40 km at 30 N, transport about 30 Sv) [cited]. The interior Sverdrup flow is toward the equator where the curl is negative (subtropical gyre) and poleward where positive (subpolar gyre). Near the zonal boundaries (500 to 1,000 km wide in the model) the meridional transport falls to zero.
- Stommel's linear-friction width is r/beta (r a bottom drag rate, 1/(15 days) in R1451's model) [cited form]; with that r it is 39 km at beta_0 [derived]. The width sets the gyre's asymmetry (western intensification) and, in the model, the transport of the boundary current follows the interior.
- Time scales: baroclinic long Rossby waves cross at -beta C^2/f^2, 3 km/day in the subtropics, so the Sverdrup interior takes about 3 to 5 years to spin up in an Earth basin of thousands of km; the barotropic response takes weeks (1,200 km/day at 30 N); an annual cycle of stress ±50% moves the western boundary transport only about ±4% [cited]. On the planet beta is 3.2 times larger (6.3e-11 at 30 N against 2.0e-11), so the crossing of a 2,000 km basin takes about half a year [derived]: use a climatological steady state, no memory, the western boundary current as a sub-grid closure [design].

### What changes on a 2,000 km planet [derived]

- The same stress would give a curl 3.2 times larger across a latitude band 3.2 times narrower, but beta is also 3.2 times larger; for a basin 2,000 km wide and curl scaled by 1/a the Sverdrup transport is 6.9 Sv (against 21 Sv for a 6,000 km Earth basin with the same curl), and unscaled it is 2.2 Sv. A gyre of a few Sv is a small ocean heat transporter.
- The boundary layer: Stommel's r/beta is 12 km (against 39), shorter than a 12.3 km cell; R_d is the same 40 km as on Earth (f and C unchanged), 3 cells, so the boundary current is at the edge of what tier 0 resolves. Treat it as a flux on the west face of the basin: all of -N from the interior [design].
- The 75 m mixed layer's seasonal swing, the air's advection speed and so on are unchanged; only the basin widths (2,000 to 5,000 km) change the fetch.

### Sea-surface temperature and why west coasts are mild (R1450, R1437)

- The finding [cited]: the winter contrast between western Europe and eastern North America, up to 15 C and more, "is hardly affected by" ocean heat transport in two atmospheric GCMs with mixed-layer oceans run with and without it; it is due to advection by the mean westerlies and stationary waves (south-westerlies bring maritime air to Europe, north-westerlies bring continental air to north-eastern America) and to the winter release of heat stored in summer by the ocean mixed layer, not to the Gulf Stream. The effect of the mountains (the Rockies) accounts for about half of the contrast; the rest is the zonal-mean westerlies plus thermally forced stationary waves. Even the Pacific coast of Canada and Alaska is 5 to 7 C colder than the Atlantic coast of Europe with or without ocean transport (the Icelandic low sits further north and east than the Aleutian low).
- Heat transports [cited, R1450]: the atmosphere carries about 5 times the ocean's poleward heat in midlatitudes, the two equal near 15 N and S; the annual atmospheric transport across 35 N is about 6 PW in winter (54 W/m^2 over the area north of there) and the global ocean's 1.3 PW; with a sensitivity of about 2 W/m^2/K the atmosphere warms the area north of 35 N by 27 C and the ocean by about 6 C (4.5 C with sea ice fixed). The ocean's share is zonally uniform over land, and large over the Atlantic and near the Norwegian and Barents Seas where sea ice retreats.
- Heat carried by a western boundary current of 30 Sv and a temperature difference of 7 K is rho c_p N dT = 0.86 PW [derived with rho 1,025, c_p 3,990], the order of the 1.3 PW of R1450. On this planet N is about 7 Sv, so about 0.2 PW out of a total area that is 10 times smaller.

### A minimal law for coastal temperature [design, from the above]

1. Sea-surface temperature at each month from the energy balance's ocean column: a mixed layer of 75 m (C_W = 4.7 B years, R1437, 3.1e8 J/m^2/K), forced by insolation and the atmosphere's meridional transport, with the ocean's own transport as a correction from the gyre (the western boundary current warms the poleward-flowing western side by dT = (heat flux)/(rho c_p N) over its length). The seasonal swing at the sea is F/(29.5 B), 1/21 of the land's (atmosphere file, section 2).
2. The air temperature at a coast is the surface's, blended by the fetch of the wind over each surface: T_air(x) = T_sea + (T_land - T_sea) (1 - exp(-L_land / lambda)), L_land the upwind distance over land, lambda the e-fold length of adjustment of the lowest kilometre. A first estimate is lambda = rho c_p H U / h with a boundary layer H of 1 km, U of 7 m/s, and an exchange h of 15 W/m^2/K, which gives 560 km [INFERENCE: h, H and the form are not from a source read]. Do not fit lambda to the validation facts below; if the check misses, that is a gap.
3. Wind from the sea onto the coast (the westerlies of mid-latitudes, the trades' easterlies at the east coast of a continent) gives the mild, wet coast; wind from the land (the continental side) gives the cold one. The west coast at 55 to 65 degrees on this planet (atmosphere file) has the westerlies and the sea at its back.
4. Eastern-boundary effects: where the surface wind blows equatorward along a west coast, Ekman transport M_E = tau_along / (rho f) carries the surface water offshore and cool water rises [derived from the Ekman relation in R1451, not otherwise read]; for tau 0.05 Pa at 30 degrees, M_E = 0.05 / (1,025 x 7.3e-5) = 0.67 m^2/s, which over an offshore width of 50 km is an upwelling of about 1 m/day [derived]; the cooling it makes is not sized here [gap].
5. Sea ice where the sea would fall below its freezing point, taken as -1.8 C [UNVERIFIED; the usual value for a salinity of 35], thickness by the Stefan law h = sqrt(2 k (T_f - T_s) t / (rho L)) with k 2 W/m/K, rho 1,000, L 320 kJ/kg (the values of exercise 4.37, R1411): 0.30 m after 10 days at -10 C, 0.94 m after 100 days, 1.7 m after 150 days at -20 C, 2.5 m after 200 days at -30 C (ice's own k 2.2, 917 and 334 kJ/kg, which I did not read, would raise these by 7%) [derived]. Ice cover changes the albedo: ice-free co-albedo 0.70 and ice-covered 0.38 (R1437), with the ice line at the -10 C isotherm in the Budyko-Sellers balance; sea ice insulates the ocean's heat from the air, which is what makes the air of the Norwegian and Barents Seas so much colder when ocean transport is removed and sea ice spreads (R1450: 6 C of area-mean warming against 4.5 C with ice held fixed).

### Land-sea contrast on a small planet

With 29% land on a sphere 12,600 km round, no land point is more than about 2,500 km from the sea, and the two coasts of a 4,000 km continent are about a week apart by a 7 m/s wind (6.6 days). The land's own seasonal swing (21 times the sea's) is then expressed only in the interior of the largest continents [INFERENCE from the numbers above]. Mild winters at 55 to 65 degrees on west coasts follow from fetch, and the validation file's contrast (Tofino against Regina) will be a weaker test on this planet.

## What a kernel needs (Unit 5)

Per ocean cell (a monthly climatology): wind stress (Pa, Q16) from the atmosphere file's winds with the drag law; curl by the sphere grid's metric (Q24 per metre of the stress); Sverdrup transport M_y integrated west to east from the eastern boundary (a sweep along each latitude row, the grid's face-edge halo as the carry: integer, Q16 m^2/s); the western boundary closure as a flux on the western coast cell of each basin row; the mixed-layer temperature (Q8 K); sea-ice thickness (Q8 m); coastal air temperature by a sweep along the wind. The sea level of a given epoch (Unit 6) changes which cells are ocean and the basin widths.

## Open

1. The effect of the ocean on the planet's coasts is small by the cited finding; the minimum law above (mixed layer, fetch, a boundary-current correction, sea ice) is a working design, not a source's. The e-fold length lambda, the upwelling cooling and the sea ice's thermal feedbacks are gaps.
2. The ocean's meridional overturning (thermohaline) is not modelled and no source is read for its size on a small planet; deep water formation needs sea ice and brine.
3. Tides, waves and storm surge are not included (the window's shore needs them for its beach; a separate topic).
4. Sverdrup's theory assumes flat bottom and no stratification; on a planet with continental shelves 100 to 400 km wide and a 12 km grid the shelf is 8 to 30 cells, with no shelf dynamics here.
5. Upwelling on west coasts makes cold coastal water at 20 to 40 degrees (not sized here) and so a cold dry coast with fog in the Hadley subsidence belt: that matches Earth's Atacama and Namib, which the validation file lists as dry-belt facts.
