# Nomads research: terrain, art direction and the playable prototype

Everything researched and decided while building the island generator, choosing an art style and putting it on the live sim. Branch history: island-gen (generator), island-3d (styles and play page).

## Contents
- `generator/`: the physically based island generator write-up (uplift, stream-power erosion, climate, water, soils, cover) with its layer images. Code: src/terrain.
- `artbook/index.html`: the art direction process for "Expedition Morning": brief, reference board, pillars, palette and density exploration, value checks, style guide, critique rounds, finals and loops.
- `briefs/artbook.md`: art direction brief, references and pillars.
- `briefs/styles-brief.md`: the shared brief for the round of from-scratch style mocks (demo/styles/*).
- `briefs/play-brief.md`: contracts for running the sim in the browser under the isopixel renderer.
- `briefs/gamemap.md` and `briefs/isomap.md`: maps of the game (sim, wire shapes, client) and of the isopixel renderer.

## Timeline and decisions
1. Generator: realistic erosion island, then gentled to 250 to 450 m peaks. Unchanged since island-gen.
2. Style search: several rounds of mocks; recolor-only rounds were rejected, so each style changed its representation. The operator picked isometric pixel art (F).
3. Art direction: industry process (brief, references, pillars, exploration grid, grayscale and squint checks, style guide, paintover critiques). Direction: Transport Tycoon clarity, turquoise shallows, low morning sun, a landmark in every view, small SimCity people, no UI.
4. Play page (demo/styles/play): the real sim runs in the browser with the random brain (no server, no Jev cost). Renderer bakes world-anchored chunks in workers, composes live objects at 60 fps, smooth zoom over nine levels, eight-bearing orbit, day and night palette.
5. Real objects: every tree, rock, plant and animal (about 850k things, 500 animals) is a sim object with continuous positions and inspect data; walking is about 2 m per tick.
6. Fidelity: vertical exaggeration from the true slope distribution (1.5; 3.2 made needles); one shared groundClass and waterAt for sim, renderer and inspector; continuous camera with a torture test; memory bounded near 1.3 GB.
7. Look: ground painted from the generator's continuous fields; terrain light owns value with cast shadows, so the landform reads through forests.
8. Turning: the flat image warp and cross-fade between two baked bearings leaned every tree and showed a double image mid-turn. Now every art pixel goes back into the world by its depth (for ground gy + z = 2H(u + v); the bake sends each object pixel's column from its sprite's anchor) and is projected at the bearing in between, so relief turns in 3D and sprites stay upright and whole. The GPU (demo/styles/isopixel/gpu.js) splats both bearings' baked chunks as one-pixel points with depth tests, fills what a turn uncovers from the other bearing and then from nearby ground, and crossfades their shading in an ordered dither.
9. Responsiveness: turns used to wait for the next bearing to bake (seconds at a fresh spot) and every frame composed the whole view on the CPU (about 11 ms at 1080p), so zoom and turn felt laggy although headless fps read 60. Now turns never wait: a bearing still baking is drawn from its baked neighbour turned into place, in the neighbour's shading, and its own shading dithers in once it is whole. That stand-in tears on steep ground and in dense woods, where the neighbour never saw what the turn uncovers, so the page opens only once its opening view is baked from all eight bearings, and every bearing bakes right after the view wherever the camera goes. Baked chunks live on the GPU and every frame is drawn there; the CPU only composes the 32 px tiles people, animals, fire and labels touch. Chunks still baking show other levels of the same bearing, finer first, dithered between texels when blown up. Measured at 1080p on an i9-9880H with an AMD Radeon Pro 5500M: frame cost 11.6 to 1.9 ms, every turn lands in its 380 ms, and 57 fps through turns with the CPU throttled 4x (was 20). The badge top right says whether WebGL2 draws, on which GPU, or why the CPU does; without WebGL2 a turn shows the nearer bearing until it lands.
10. Sunlight: with WebGL2 the sun moves with the sim's clock (up 5:30, down 20:30, through the south at 55 degrees) and the GPU casts its shadows, so a mountain's shadow stretches across the valley toward sunset and light falls through the gaps in the woods. The workers bake no cast shadows then. The GPU traces which ground sees the sun over the island's heights (12.5 m, a few stripes a frame while the sun moves), and lays every shadow-casting sprite as its rows, each a round slab flattened 2:1 and swept along the sun; flat cards cast only a line when the sun comes from the side. Both darken through the palette's own shadow colours in the ordered dither. The baked hillshade still comes from the screen's left at every bearing, so on a morning at bearing 0 slope light and cast shadows disagree. Without WebGL2 the bake casts its fixed shadows as before. `?hour=` pins the hour drawn (`play.sim.hour` from the console). Cost at 1080p on the Radeon Pro 5500M: about 0.2 ms of GPU time a frame, turns and zooms as fast as before with the CPU throttled 4x.
11. Turning far out, and where the time goes. Turns at island zoom showed two artifacts. The sea swam in moiré, its fine positional dither resampled onto a turned grid; now the bake marks still open water with its tone on the water ramp and the turn paints it again on the target grid, the same dither the view lands on (the sea beyond the island map, which had no depth, sits at sea level with its tone too). Specks of land flew off the coasts: the bake found each sprite pixel's anchor by object id, and ids wrap past 65000 objects a chunk, which island chunks exceed, so some trees turned about another tree's anchor; now blit records each pixel's column. Frames are cheap: at 1080p about 0.6 ms of the CPU and 1.2 ms of GPU, 60 fps through a fast zoom with the CPU throttled 4x. What arrives late in a quick zoom is chunks: about 200 to 350 ms each on a worker, 70% of it the shared groundClass rule (flora.ts) and the shore distance beside it. Water used to be painted eight times to find its animated pixels and now once where nothing in its loop can move, so chunks with water bake 1.6 to 3 times faster, and the view a zoom will land on bakes first. Moving the terrain painter onto the GPU would make chunks near instant, at the cost of a second copy of the ground rule that the sim, the inspector and the drawing now share.
12. Day and night from the sky. The palette was graded by an hour table that darkened from 19:00 while the sun still cast full shadows until 20:30, and slopes kept a fixed light from the screen's left. Now one sky lights everything (demo/styles/isopixel/live.js lightOf). The sun and moon move on a real sky near 52 degrees north with the sun 17 degrees north of the equator: up at 5:30 in the east-north-east, through the south at 55 degrees at 13:00, down at 20:30 in the west-north-west, civil twilight ending 44 minutes after sunset; the moon waxes over 29.5 days and a summer full moon rides low. The ground's light is the sun's, reddened by the air it crosses, the sky's, which falls with the sun and through twilight turns golden, rose and then blue, a glow low in the sun's quarter after sunset, moonlight, and fire. An eye adaptation shows it: flat ground at noon is the palette as drawn, the golden hour stays bright, the dark comes after sunset, and a night reads at about a third of the day, greyer and bluer except in firelight. The GPU gives every pixel a row of the light table (16 steps of the key light at the fire's four levels, and the palette as drawn for flames) by its slope toward the sun or moon over the island's heights, less shadow, and ground and the baked canopies on it take their palette tone from that slope as the bake's hillshade chose them (pal.js TONE), so relief keeps the art's hue shifts and turns with the sun. The moon casts shadows by night, and cloud from the sim's weather softens all of it. The workers bake only occlusion; without WebGL2 they bake the fixed hillshade and shadows as before and the CPU takes the sky's light on flat ground. Cost is unchanged: about 2.3 ms of GPU a still frame and 6.6 ms a turned one at 2400x1350, 57 to 58 fps through turns and zooms with the CPU throttled 4x, and 0.3 ms to rebuild the light table once a game minute.
